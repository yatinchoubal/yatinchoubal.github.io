Platform and developer experience work is some of the most important product work in a technology company, and some of the hardest to explain in an interview. Your users are often internal engineers. Your product might be a build pipeline, an SDK, an internal portal, or a set of tools that nobody outside the company will ever see. When a recruiter, a hiring manager from a different domain, or an executive asks "So what did you build?", it is easy to drift into jargon or undersell the work.

I lead developer experience product work in games today, so this topic is close to me. I won't get into specifics of any employer's systems here. The principles below are general, and they apply whether your platform serves game developers, payments engineers, or data scientists.

## Why this work is hard to explain

A few things make platform stories tricky:

- **The user is indirect.** You improve life for engineers, who then improve life for customers.
- **The impact is often prevented pain.** Fewer outages, fewer blocked builds, less waiting. Absence of problems is hard to picture.
- **The vocabulary is specialized.** Terms like "CI throughput," "golden paths," or "service mesh" mean nothing to many interviewers.
- **The work is shared.** Platform outcomes usually involve many teams, so your individual role needs to be clear.

None of this means the work is less valuable. It just means you need to translate.

## The translation ladder

I find it helpful to explain platform work as a ladder with three rungs. Start at the top for nontechnical audiences and go down only when asked.

1. **Business outcome:** What did the company get? Faster delivery, more reliable releases, lower cost, happier teams.
2. **User outcome:** What changed for the engineers or teams you served? Less waiting, fewer manual steps, clearer documentation.
3. **What you built or changed:** The tool, process, or system, described in one plain sentence.

Most people tell the story bottom up, starting with the technology. Flipping the order lets a nontechnical interviewer understand why it mattered before they hear how.

## Before and after (hypothetical)

**Before:** "I owned the roadmap for our internal CI/CD platform and drove adoption of containerized build agents with improved caching across multiple service teams."

**After:** "Engineers were spending a lot of their day waiting for code to build and test, which slowed down every feature the company shipped. I led the product work to make that process faster and more predictable. We changed how builds were run and reused work between them. Teams told us they could ship changes more often, and wait times dropped noticeably based on our dashboards."

The second version still contains the substance. It just leads with the problem and the people affected.

## Talking about impact honestly

This is where platform PMs are often tempted to stretch. Because platform impact is indirect, it is easy to claim credit for a revenue number or a launch that your platform only supported. Don't. Interviewers notice, and follow-up questions will expose it.

Instead, use the evidence you actually have:

- **Real metrics you can stand behind:** adoption, build times, incident counts, support tickets, satisfaction surveys.
- **Labeled estimates:** "I estimated roughly X hours saved per engineer per week, based on before-and-after sampling. It wasn't a formal study."
- **Qualitative outcomes:** "Teams stopped escalating build problems to leadership," or "New engineers could make their first change without asking for help."

If you don't have numbers, qualitative impact described specifically is completely legitimate. And if a metric is confidential, say so plainly: "I can't share the exact figures, but the direction was clearly positive and the program was expanded." That is a normal, professional answer.

### Protecting confidential information

Platform work often touches internal architecture, security practices, vendor contracts, and unreleased plans. Keep those details out of interviews, resumes, and portfolios. Describe the type of problem and your approach, not the internal specifics. If you are unsure whether something is shareable, leave it out.

## Showing product judgment, not just technical depth

Nontechnical interviewers are often assessing whether you think like a product manager. Make sure your story includes:

- [ ] How you learned what your internal users needed (interviews, surveys, usage data, sitting with teams)
- [ ] How you decided what to build first, and what you deferred
- [ ] How you drove adoption, since internal tools often aren't mandatory
- [ ] How you measured success, honestly
- [ ] How you handled competing requests from different teams

Adoption is especially worth highlighting. Getting busy engineers to switch tools requires the same skills as external product marketing: understanding pain, reducing friction, and earning trust.

## A one-minute answer template

> "My users were [who], and the problem was [plain-language pain]. That mattered because [business consequence]. I led [your specific role] to [what changed, in one sentence]. As a result, [real metric, labeled estimate, or qualitative outcome]. The hardest part was [trade-off or adoption challenge], and I handled it by [your action]."

Practice saying this out loud to someone outside tech. If they can repeat the gist back to you, you are in good shape.

## On your resume

The same ladder works on paper. Lead each bullet with the outcome, then the action. Replace internal tool names with a short description ("internal build system" rather than a codename). Keep numbers only where you can explain them in an interview.
