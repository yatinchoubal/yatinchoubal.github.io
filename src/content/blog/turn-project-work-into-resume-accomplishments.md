Many resumes from product managers and technical professionals have the same problem. They describe work, not results. "Led the migration of the billing platform." "Owned the roadmap for developer tools." "Partnered with engineering on API redesign." All true, probably. But none of it tells a hiring manager what changed because you were there.

This post walks through how I turn project descriptions into accomplishments. It also covers what to do when you don't have tidy numbers, because that's the situation most people are actually in.

## Start with the project, then ask what changed

A project is something you worked on. An accomplishment is what was different afterward. To get from one to the other, I use four questions:

1. **What was the problem or opportunity?** What was broken, slow, risky, or missing?
2. **What did you specifically do?** Not the team. You. Decisions, tradeoffs, alignment, analysis.
3. **What changed as a result?** For users, the business, the team, or the system.
4. **How do you know?** What evidence exists, even if it's informal?

Write the answers in plain sentences first. Don't worry about resume formatting yet. The goal is to get the raw material out of your head.

## Where the evidence usually lives

People often tell me they "don't have metrics." Usually they have more than they think. Before you decide a project can't be quantified, check these places:

- Launch announcements, release notes, or internal demos
- Dashboards you or your team looked at regularly
- Quarterly business reviews or planning documents
- Support ticket volumes or themes before and after
- Survey results, NPS comments, or user interview notes
- Your own performance reviews and peer feedback
- Sprint or delivery data (cycle time, number of releases, incidents)

Only use what you can stand behind in an interview. If you can't explain where a number came from, it doesn't belong on your resume. And never share figures your employer considers confidential. Percentages, ranges, or relative language often work just as well.

## A simple bullet structure

Once you have the raw answers, compress them into one line. The structure I use most:

**Action verb + what you did + for whom or why + result or evidence**

You don't need every part every time, but the result should almost always be there.

### Before and after (hypothetical examples)

These are illustrative examples I made up to show the pattern. The numbers are placeholders, not real results.

- **Before:** Owned roadmap for internal developer portal.
- **After:** Reprioritized developer portal roadmap around onboarding friction identified in user interviews, cutting new-hire setup time from roughly two days to under half a day (team estimate).

- **Before:** Led API redesign project with engineering.
- **After:** Drove API redesign with three engineering teams, consolidating overlapping endpoints and reducing integration-related support tickets by about a third over two quarters.

- **Before:** Worked on checkout improvements.
- **After:** Identified a drop-off point in checkout through funnel analysis and shipped a simplified flow that measurably improved completion rates in an A/B test.

Notice the third example has no number. That's fine. "Measurably improved in an A/B test" is honest and still tells the reader you used evidence.

## When you don't have numbers

This is where people get into trouble. They feel pressure to quantify everything, so they guess, round up, or borrow a team-level number and present it as their own. Please don't. Interviewers ask follow-up questions, and a made-up metric falls apart quickly. It also isn't the kind of career you want to build.

Here are honest options instead:

- **Estimate and label it.** "Roughly," "about," "an estimated," or "(team estimate)" are all fine when you have a reasonable basis.
- **Use scope instead of outcome.** Number of teams, users, services, regions, or partners involved.
- **Use relative language.** "Reduced," "eliminated," "first," "replaced a manual process."
- **Describe impact qualitatively.** "Unblocked the platform team's migration," "became the standard intake process for the org."
- **Point to adoption or reuse.** If others adopted your framework, template, or tool, say so.

Qualitative impact described clearly beats a vague number every time.

## A quick checklist before you finalize a bullet

- [ ] Does it start with a strong, specific verb (not "responsible for")?
- [ ] Is it clear what *I* did, separate from the team?
- [ ] Is there a result, outcome, or piece of evidence?
- [ ] Could I explain every number and its source in an interview?
- [ ] Are estimates labeled as estimates?
- [ ] Have I avoided anything confidential?
- [ ] Is it one or two lines, not a paragraph?

## Pick fewer, stronger bullets

You don't need eight bullets per role. For most product and technical roles, three to five strong accomplishments per recent position is plenty. Pick the ones that best match the jobs you're targeting. A senior PM role cares about strategy, cross-functional influence, and outcomes. A technical PM role also wants to see systems thinking and comfort with engineering tradeoffs. Choose accordingly.

One more tip: keep a running "evidence log" going forward. A short note each month about what shipped, what changed, and how you know will save you hours the next time you update your resume or write a self-evaluation.
