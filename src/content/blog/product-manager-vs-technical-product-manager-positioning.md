People moving between these roles often ask what the difference is. The honest answer is that the line between them varies a lot by company. One company's "technical PM" is another company's "platform PM," and some organizations don't make the distinction at all.

Still, the distinction matters for how you position yourself. If you're applying to both kinds of roles with the same resume and the same stories, you're probably underselling yourself for at least one of them.

## What tends to differ

These are general patterns, not rules. Always read the specific job description.

**Product manager roles** usually emphasize:

- Understanding customers and market problems
- Defining what to build and why
- Prioritizing across business goals, user needs, and constraints
- Measuring outcomes like adoption, retention, conversion, or satisfaction
- Working closely with design, marketing, sales, and support

**Technical product manager roles** usually emphasize:

- Products whose users are often engineers, internal teams, or other systems
- Platforms, APIs, infrastructure, data, or developer tools
- Comfort discussing architecture, dependencies, and technical tradeoffs
- Outcomes like reliability, developer productivity, performance, or platform adoption
- Close partnership with engineering leaders and architects

Both roles require product judgment. Technical PM is not "PM minus customers." The customers are just often technical, and the tradeoffs often live closer to the system.

## Figure out where your experience actually points

Before you reposition anything, take an honest inventory. Ask yourself:

- [ ] Who were the users of the things I worked on? External customers, internal teams, developers?
- [ ] When I made decisions, were the hardest tradeoffs about user value, business value, or technical feasibility?
- [ ] Which conversations did I lead comfortably: customer research, pricing, roadmap reviews, architecture reviews?
- [ ] What outcomes did I track or influence?
- [ ] What kind of work gave me energy, and what drained me?

If you're coming from engineering, solutions architecture, or another technical role, you may lean toward technical PM roles at first. That's a reasonable path. But don't assume it's your only path. Many people with technical backgrounds are strong customer-facing PMs too.

## Same experience, two framings

You don't need different experience to apply for both role types. You need to emphasize different parts of the same true story.

### Before and after (hypothetical example)

Imagine a platform PM who led a project to consolidate several internal authentication services into one. These bullets are illustrative, and any numbers are placeholders.

**Generic version:**
- Led authentication consolidation project across multiple teams.

**Framed for a technical PM role:**
- Led consolidation of four internal authentication services into a single platform, partnering with architects on migration sequencing and reducing integration effort for consuming teams (team estimate of roughly 30 percent less setup work).

**Framed for a product manager role:**
- Identified sign-in friction as a recurring theme in user feedback and led a cross-team effort to unify authentication, simplifying the login experience for end users and reducing related support contacts.

Both versions describe the same project. Neither invents anything. They just lead with what each audience cares about most. If you only have evidence for one framing, use that one. Don't stretch a project to fit a role it doesn't really support.

## Position honestly on your resume

A few practical guidelines:

- **Match your summary to the role type.** One or two lines that name the kind of products you build and who uses them.
- **Reorder bullets per application.** Put the most relevant accomplishments first under each role.
- **Use the language of the role, truthfully.** If you did run architecture discussions, say so. If you mainly consumed architecture decisions, don't imply you led them.
- **Keep your titles accurate.** If your title was "Program Manager" but your work was product-heavy, keep the real title and let the bullets show the product work.
- **Label estimates.** And if you don't have numbers, describe impact qualitatively. "Became the default integration path for new services" is a real outcome.

## Position honestly in interviews

Interviewers for each role type will probe different things.

For **PM roles**, expect questions like:

- How did you decide what problem to solve?
- How did you know users wanted this?
- How did you measure success?

For **technical PM roles**, expect questions like:

- Walk me through the system and its dependencies.
- What technical tradeoffs did you weigh?
- How did you work with engineering when you disagreed on approach?

Prepare a few stories that you can tell from both angles. Know the technical details well enough to go one level deeper than your resume, and know the user or business impact well enough to explain why the work mattered.

## If you're still unsure which to target

That's normal. A few ways to get clearer:

1. Read ten job descriptions for each role type and highlight what excites you and what doesn't.
2. Talk with people in both roles at companies you're interested in.
3. Look at which of your past accomplishments you most want to talk about. That's a useful signal.
4. Consider applying to both, with tailored materials, and see which conversations feel like a better fit.
