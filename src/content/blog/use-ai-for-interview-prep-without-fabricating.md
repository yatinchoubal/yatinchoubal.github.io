AI tools can be genuinely useful for interview preparation. They can generate practice questions, point out gaps in your structure, and help you say something more clearly. They can also quietly make your preparation worse. The risk isn't that AI is bad. The risk is that it is very good at producing confident, polished content that isn't true about you.

My position is straightforward. Use AI to sharpen your real experience, never to replace it. Here is how I'd draw the line.

## Good uses of AI in interview prep

### Generating practice questions

Paste in a job description (a public one, not an internal document) and ask for likely questions across product strategy, prioritization, execution, and stakeholder management. Ask for follow-up questions too. This helps you see the range of what might come up and spot topics where you don't have a ready story.

### Critiquing structure

Write your answer in your own words first. Then ask the tool to critique it: Is the problem clear? Is my role clear? Did I name a trade-off? Is the outcome specific? This is one of the best uses because the content stays yours and the tool only acts as an editor.

### Tightening wording

Long, rambling answers are common. AI can help you cut filler, shorten a two-minute answer to ninety seconds, or replace jargon with plain language. Review every edit to make sure it didn't change the facts or add claims.

### Role-play

Ask the tool to act as a skeptical hiring manager and push on your answer with follow-up questions. This is useful for building comfort going two or three layers deep. Treat it as practice, not prediction.

## Bad uses of AI in interview prep

### Inventing stories or metrics

This is the line I care about most. Do not ask an AI tool to "write a story about a time I led a cross-functional launch" if that story didn't happen the way it describes. Do not let it fill in numbers you don't actually know. Fabricated stories fall apart under follow-up questions, and they can damage trust permanently if discovered later, including after you are hired.

If you don't have a precise metric, say so. Use an estimate you can explain and label it as an estimate, or describe the impact qualitatively. "I don't have the exact number, but support tickets on that issue dropped noticeably after the change" is a perfectly good answer.

### Pasting confidential employer documents into tools

Internal strategy decks, roadmaps, performance data, customer information, and code should not go into a general AI tool. Depending on the tool and its settings, inputs may be stored, reviewed, or used in ways you don't control. Many employers have explicit policies about this, and breaking them can create real problems for you.

### Memorizing AI scripts

Answers memorized word for word from an AI draft tend to sound rehearsed and generic. Interviewers notice when an answer is smooth but lacks texture. Use AI to clarify your points, then practice speaking from bullet notes, not a script.

## Privacy cautions checklist

Before you paste anything into an AI tool, check:

- [ ] Does this include any employer-confidential information (internal names, numbers, plans, customers)?
- [ ] Does it include anyone else's personal information?
- [ ] Do I know whether this tool stores or trains on my input, and have I checked its settings?
- [ ] Does my current employer have a policy on AI tool usage?
- [ ] Could I describe this situation in general terms instead?

When in doubt, abstract it. "A B2B payments product with a large enterprise customer" is enough context for the tool to help with structure.

## Before and after (hypothetical)

**Risky prompt:** "Write me a strong answer about a time I increased conversion by 30% through a pricing experiment."

(If that didn't happen, or you don't know the real number, this produces a fabrication.)

**Better prompt:** "Here is my draft answer about a pricing experiment I ran. I don't have the final conversion number, only that results were positive enough to roll out. Critique the structure, flag anything vague, and suggest where I could be clearer about my role. Don't add any facts or numbers."

The second prompt keeps the AI in the editor role, states the truth about the evidence, and explicitly tells the tool not to invent anything.

## A disclosure mindset

You don't need to announce in every interview that you practiced with AI. But I'd suggest a simple test: if an interviewer asked how you prepared, would you be comfortable explaining? "I used an AI tool to generate practice questions and critique my structure, and I wrote the stories myself" is an answer most people would respect.

The same goes for written materials. If a company asks you not to use AI on a take-home exercise, follow that. If it is allowed, use it in a way you could describe openly. Everything you say in an interview should be something you can stand behind and expand on without notes.

## A simple workflow

1. Write your stories yourself from real experience and real evidence.
2. Use AI to generate practice questions and identify gaps.
3. Ask AI to critique structure and clarity, with an instruction not to add facts.
4. Review every suggestion against what actually happened.
5. Role-play with AI to practice follow-ups.
6. Practice out loud from notes, ideally with a real person giving feedback.
