Most people I talk to about promotion have the same frustration. They are told they need to "show more leadership," but nobody explains what that looks like when you don't manage anyone yet.

My view is simple. Leadership before the title is mostly about taking responsibility for outcomes that are bigger than your own task list, and making that responsibility visible in a way other people can verify.

## What decision makers are actually looking for

When a manager or promotion committee asks whether someone is "operating at the next level," they are usually asking a few practical questions:

- Does this person see problems before they become escalations?
- Can they get people who don't report to them to move in the same direction?
- Do they make decisions with incomplete information and explain their reasoning?
- Do other people get better because this person is on the team?
- Can I trust them with something ambiguous without checking in every day?

None of these require a direct report.

## Five behaviors that read as leadership

### 1. Own a problem no one has claimed

Every team has a gap that falls between roles. A flaky release process. A metric nobody trusts. A recurring customer complaint that gets passed around. Pick one, tell your manager you want to own it, and define what "fixed" means.

For example, imagine a technical PM who notices that every quarterly planning cycle stalls because teams use different definitions of "committed." Writing a one-page definition, getting it reviewed, and running the next cycle with it is leadership. It is also easy to describe later because it has a clear before and after.

### 2. Write things down so others can decide

Clear written thinking scales your influence beyond the meetings you attend. A short decision memo with options, trade-offs, and a recommendation gives leaders something to react to.

### 3. Disagree well, then commit

Raising a concern early, with a specific reason and an alternative, is a senior behavior. So is committing fully once the decision is made. People remember who helped the team get to a decision and who relitigated it for weeks.

### 4. Make other people better

Review a peer's spec thoughtfully. Onboard a new teammate. Share a template you built. Run a short retro after a rough launch and focus on the system, not the blame. This is the closest thing to management you can do without the title, and it is often what managers cite first.

### 5. Close the loop on your own commitments

This sounds basic, but it is the foundation everything else rests on. If you say you will follow up by Thursday, follow up by Thursday. Leadership credibility is built on many small kept promises.

## A weekly leadership checklist

Use this as a short Friday review. You don't need to hit every item every week.

- [ ] Did I unblock someone outside my immediate work?
- [ ] Did I write down a decision, risk, or recommendation that others can reference?
- [ ] Did I raise a concern early instead of late?
- [ ] Did I follow through on every commitment I made this week?
- [ ] Did I learn something about a stakeholder's priorities that changes how I work with them?
- [ ] Did I capture evidence of any of the above in my own notes?

That last item matters more than people expect. If you don't record what happened while it is fresh, you will struggle to describe it honestly at review time.

## How to record leadership evidence honestly

Here is where I want to be direct. When you write up leadership work for a review, a promotion packet, or an interview, describe what really happened. Do not inflate your role, invent numbers, or claim outcomes you can't support. If you led part of something, say which part. If a result came from a team effort, say so and explain your specific contribution.

If you don't have a precise metric, that is fine. You can describe impact qualitatively ("planning reviews no longer needed a second session to resolve scope"), or give an estimate that you label as an estimate ("roughly a few hours saved per team each cycle, based on my own tracking"). Honest, specific evidence is more persuasive than an impressive number you can't defend in a follow-up question.

### A simple evidence template

> **Situation:** What was the problem, and why did it matter?
> **What I did:** The specific actions I took, including who I influenced.
> **Result:** What changed. Use real numbers if you have them, a labeled estimate if you don't, or a clear qualitative description.
> **What it shows:** The leadership behavior this demonstrates.

### Before and after (hypothetical)

**Before:** "Led cross-team planning improvements that drove massive efficiency gains."

**After:** "Noticed planning cycles stalled over inconsistent scope definitions. Drafted a shared definition, reviewed it with three team leads, and used it in the next cycle. The follow-up planning session we usually needed wasn't required. I don't have a precise time-saved figure, but leads told me the process felt clearer."

The second version is less flashy and far more credible. It invites good questions instead of skeptical ones.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Taking over instead of enabling.** Running someone else's project uninvited reads as poor judgment.
- **Doing it invisibly.** Your manager can't advocate for what they don't know about.
- **Overstating the story later.** Exaggeration gets exposed in calibration and interviews.
