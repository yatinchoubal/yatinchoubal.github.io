The annual self-evaluation is one of the few times each year you get to shape how your work is understood. Many people treat it as paperwork. They write it the night before, list what they worked on, and hope their manager remembers the rest.

Your manager probably won't remember the rest. Not because they don't care, but because they're writing several reviews, attending calibration, and trying to recall twelve months of work for each person. A clear, evidence-based self-evaluation makes their job easier and gives your work a fair hearing.

## What a good self-evaluation does

A strong self-evaluation:

- Connects your work to goals your team or organization cared about
- Shows what changed because of your work, not just what you did
- Backs claims with evidence your manager can verify
- Acknowledges what didn't go well and what you learned
- Points toward where you want to grow next

It doesn't need to be long. It needs to be specific and true.

## Step 1: gather your evidence first

Don't start writing until you've collected your raw material. Writing from memory tends to overweight recent work and underweight the quiet, important things you did early in the year.

Places to look:

- [ ] Your goals or OKRs from the start of the year
- [ ] Launch notes, release announcements, and demo recordings
- [ ] Planning docs, decision docs, and strategy documents you wrote
- [ ] Dashboards or reports you tracked
- [ ] Messages where someone thanked you or acknowledged your work
- [ ] Feedback from peers, stakeholders, or your manager during the year
- [ ] Your calendar (it's a surprisingly good record of what you spent time on)
- [ ] Any running notes or evidence log you kept

Pull the relevant pieces into one document, with links where you can.

## Step 2: group your work into a few themes

Instead of listing every project, group your work into three to five themes. These might map to your goals, to your company's competency areas, or to the major problems you worked on.

For example, a product manager's themes might be:

1. Delivering a major platform initiative
2. Improving how the team makes prioritization decisions
3. Building relationships with a new partner organization
4. Mentoring a newer PM

Themes help your manager see the shape of your year, and they make it easier to discuss in calibration.

## Step 3: write each theme with a simple structure

For each theme, I suggest this structure:

> **Goal or context:** What was the problem or expectation?
> **What I did:** Two to four specific actions, especially decisions and tradeoffs.
> **Impact:** What changed, and how I know.
> **What I learned or would do differently:** One or two honest lines.

### Before and after (hypothetical example)

This is an illustrative example. The numbers are placeholders.

**Before:**
"Worked on the developer onboarding project. Collaborated with many teams. It went well and got good feedback."

**After:**
"Goal: reduce the time new engineers spent getting set up. I ran interviews with recent hires to find the biggest blockers, prioritized three fixes with the tooling team, and wrote the rollout plan. Based on a follow-up survey, setup time dropped from roughly two days to about half a day (self-reported estimate). Two other orgs have since asked to reuse the onboarding checklist. What I'd do differently: involve security earlier, since their review added a few weeks we could have planned for."

The second version is specific, labels its estimate, includes a qualitative signal of impact (reuse by other orgs), and is honest about a misstep.

## Be honest about evidence

Self-evaluations can pull people toward overstatement. Resist it. An inflated claim can undermine the rest of your review.

Some guardrails:

- **Only use numbers you can source.** If someone asks "where did this come from," you should have an answer.
- **Label estimates.** "Roughly," "an estimated," or "based on a survey of the team" are all fine.
- **Separate your contribution from the team's.** Say "I" for what you did and "we" for shared results.
- **Describe impact qualitatively when you don't have numbers.** "Unblocked the migration," "became the team's standard process," and "reduced escalations to my manager" are real outcomes.
- **Don't omit the hard parts.** A brief, honest note about what didn't work shows judgment and self-awareness.

## Step 4: close with growth and what's next

End with a short section about where you want to grow. This is especially important if you're hoping to be considered for promotion. Be specific:

- What skills or scope do you want to build next year?
- Where have you already started operating at the next level? (Include evidence.)
- What support would help?

This gives your manager something concrete to advocate for.

## A quick final checklist

- [ ] Did I gather evidence before writing?
- [ ] Are my themes connected to goals the organization cared about?
- [ ] Does each theme include impact, not just activity?
- [ ] Are all numbers sourced, and estimates labeled?
- [ ] Is it clear what I did versus the team?
- [ ] Did I include at least one honest lesson?
- [ ] Is there a clear growth section?
- [ ] Have I kept anything confidential out of documents that might be widely shared?
